# Flood Playbook Note — Source of Water Interpretation
## Decoding "such as rivers, lakes and the sea" — Why Same Words Produce Different Outcomes

**Derived from:** FLOOD-035 (DRN-4415847, Wakam, Dec 2023) and FLOOD-038 (DRN-4948332, Aviva, Mar 2025)  
**Applicable to:** All policies using illustrative flood source definitions  
**Status:** Active — two-case precedent pair confirmed

---

## 1. The Policy Language

Both cases involved policies that defined (or described) flood using essentially the same phrase:

> *"An overflow of external water sources, such as rivers, lakes, and the sea."*

In FLOOD-035 the water came from a **neighbour's canopy gutter**. The claim was **covered**.  
In FLOOD-038 the water came from a **rising water table**. The claim was **not covered**.

Same words. Opposite outcomes. The distinction is physical, not linguistic.

---

## 2. The Two-Part Test

The definition contains two separable requirements:

| Requirement | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **"Overflow"** | The water must move from a higher level downward to a level where it should not be — it overflows its normal confines |
| **"External water source"** | The origin of the water must be a discrete identifiable external source that contains, accumulates, or channels water |

The word **"such as"** tells us the list that follows (rivers, lakes, sea) is **illustrative, not exhaustive**. This was confirmed in FLOOD-035. But the non-exhaustiveness of the list does not dissolve the two core requirements: **overflow** and **external water source**. The "such as" language expands *what qualifies as a source*, not *what qualifies as a mechanism*.

---

## 3. Source-of-Water Analysis — The Physical Distinction

### 3a. Above-Ground External Overflow (COVERED — FLOOD-035)

**Scenario:** Water from a neighbour's defective porch canopy gutter overflowed and ingressed into Ms K's wall.

**Analysis:**
- The canopy is an **identifiable discrete external structure** that accumulates and channels water
- The water **overflowed** from that structure — it moved from where it was contained to where it should not be
- The source is **above ground** and **external to the claimant's property**
- The mechanism is **overflow** in the ordinary sense of the word

**Result:** Fits both requirements. Covered under flood definition.

**FOS reasoning (FLOOD-035):**  
> *"I think it does [qualify], as it's clearly external (as it's from the neighbour's property). It obviously is water and a source."*

The "such as" list is non-exhaustive — rivers, lakes, and sea are illustrations of surface water sources that overflow. A neighbour's canopy is simply another such source.

---

### 3b. Rising Groundwater / Water Table (NOT COVERED — FLOOD-038)

**Scenario:** Mrs and Mr S's property flooded from a rising water table. Standing water appeared under the reception room floor. Experts confirmed the source was groundwater from an elevated water table.

**Analysis:**
- The water table is **naturally occurring subsoil water** — it exists within the earth's soil and subsoil as a permanent feature
- It does not *overflow* from a discrete identifiable source — it **rises through the soil** under hydrostatic pressure
- It is not an **external water source** in the same physical sense as a river, lake, or canopy; it is a geological phenomenon
- The mechanism is **upward percolation through soil**, not downward overflow from a container

**Result:** Fails the "overflow" requirement. Also fails the "external water source" requirement as understood in the context of surface water bodies. Not covered.

**FOS reasoning (FLOOD-038):**  
> *"The water sources described are volumes of water, which overflow at ground level, causing water to flood downwards to a lower level. The water table is water that naturally exists in the soils and subsoils, and this water rising closer to the floor of a home is not within this definition, or category of perils."*

Even acknowledging the "such as" language creates a non-exhaustive list, the water table is not merely a water source not listed — it is a categorically different physical phenomenon that does not overflow in the required sense.

---

## 4. The Non-Exhaustiveness Principle and Its Limits

The "such as" principle (confirmed in FLOOD-035) means:

**Applies to:** Any above-ground discrete external water source that accumulates water and from which water overflows onto the property. This includes:
- Rivers, lakes, sea (listed examples)
- Neighbouring canopy gutters, drainage channels, water features, ponds
- Overflowing surface drains, public drainage channels
- Any external container/structure from which water physically overflows

**Does NOT apply to:**
- Naturally occurring groundwater or water table (geological phenomenon, not a discrete source that overflows)
- Water that rises through subsoil under hydrostatic pressure
- Moisture migrating through walls by capillary action without an overflow source

The non-exhaustive list expands the *categories of surface overflow source* — it does not dissolve the requirement that water must *overflow* from a *discrete source*.

---

## 5. The Indirect Causation Problem (FLOOD-038 additional point)

Mr S argued in FLOOD-038 that even if the water table was the immediate cause, the river had exceeded its banks and saturated the ground, which raised the water table. This indirect causation argument failed for two independent reasons:

1. **Policy mechanics:** The water did not "overflow" from the river into the property — it percolated through soil. The river's overflow was not the direct cause of property damage.
2. **Policy exclusion:** Aviva's policy excluded **indirect loss**. Even where river flooding raised the water table (an indirect chain), Aviva expressly excluded such indirect causation.

**Playbook rule:** Where a property has a surface water flood exclusion and groundwater rising from below is the mechanism of damage, the claim is excluded even if a nearby river contributed to elevated groundwater levels, provided the policy excludes indirect loss.

---

## 6. Natural vs Non-Natural Water Ingress

The cases establish a useful framework:

| Source Type | Physical Mechanism | Covered? | Reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| River / sea / lake overflow | Surface overflow → enters property | Yes | Classic flood — named in definition |
| Neighbour's canopy/gutter | Above-ground external overflow → enters property | Yes | Non-exhaustive "such as"; external overflow source |
| Overflowing surface drains | Above-ground overflow → enters property at ground level | Likely yes | Same overflow mechanism as river/lake |
| Rising water table | Subsoil water rises through soil under pressure | No | Not overflow; not discrete external source; geological |
| Groundwater seepage through wall | Percolation through structure under soil moisture pressure | No | Same as water table — subsoil moisture, not overflow |
| Capillary action in walls | Moisture migration by capillary | No | Not flood overflow mechanism |

---

## 7. Contra Proferentem — When It Applies and When It Does Not

**FLOOD-035 (Wakam):** The FOS agreed that the canopy scenario was arguably within the definition AND that the definition was ambiguous (the term "source" was undefined). Because the term was genuinely ambiguous, *contra proferentem* resolved the ambiguity in Ms K's favour.

**FLOOD-038 (Aviva):** The FOS did not apply *contra proferentem*. The water table scenario was not found to be ambiguous — the physical distinction between surface overflow and subsoil groundwater was held to be clear enough. There was no genuine ambiguity requiring resolution.

**Playbook rule on contra proferentem:**  
Contra proferentem requires genuine, material ambiguity — not a strained or far-fetched alternative reading. Where the physical nature of the water source places it clearly in a different category from the listed examples (not just unlisted but categorically different), the term is not ambiguous and contra proferentem does not assist the policyholder.

---

## 8. Policy Wording Interpretation Principles — Summary

These five principles emerge from the two cases read together:

1. **"Such as" creates a non-exhaustive list.** The examples that follow are illustrations. Other sources of the same physical type are included.

2. **Non-exhaustiveness has limits.** The list is non-exhaustive within the category of *surface water sources that overflow*. It does not extend to categorically different water mechanisms (subsoil groundwater, capillary action).

3. **Contra proferentem requires genuine ambiguity.** Where a term is ambiguous and the policyholder's reading is reasonable, the insurer's narrower reading loses. Where the physical facts place the scenario outside even a generous reading, contra proferentem does not apply.

4. **Indirect causation is insufficient.** Where surface water from a listed source (river) indirectly causes groundwater to rise (water table), the indirect chain does not bring the claim within the flood definition — especially where the policy excludes indirect loss.

5. **Physical mechanism determines coverage, not origin.** The question is not "where did the water originally come from?" but "how did it arrive at the property?" Overflow from above → covered. Rise from below through soil → not covered.

## 9. AI Rule Candidates — Composite

```
IF water_source_is_above_ground_discrete_external_structure
   AND water_overflows_from_that_structure_onto_property
   AND flood_definition_uses_such_as_before_list
THEN claim_likely_covered_under_flood_peril

IF water_mechanism_is_rising_water_table_or_groundwater_percolation
   AND flood_definition_references_overflow_of_external_water_sources
THEN claim_excluded — subsoil_water_is_not_overflow_of_external_source

IF policy_wording_genuinely_ambiguous_AND_policyholder_reading_reasonable
THEN apply_contra_proferentem_in_policyholder_favour

IF river_flooding_raises_water_table_AND_policy_excludes_indirect_loss
THEN groundwater_damage_excluded_notwithstanding_river_as_original_cause

IF flood_definition_says_such_as_and_source_is_above_ground_external
THEN non_exhaustive_list_expands_to_include_that_source
```
